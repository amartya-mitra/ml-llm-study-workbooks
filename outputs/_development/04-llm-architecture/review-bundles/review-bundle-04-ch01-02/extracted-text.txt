3 Chapter 1: Transformer Refresher
From Tokens to the Next-Token Distribution

3.1 Learning objectives
By the end of this chapter you should be able to:
• State the decoder-only next-token objective and explain why it is what lets a model generate text one
piece at a time.
• Trace a single token’s vector as it moves through the residual stream, the attention sublayer, and the
feed-forward sublayer of one decoder block.
• Explain causal masking precisely – which positions a given position may and may not attend to – rather
than the imprecise “only the previous token” version.
• Distinguish prefill from a single autoregressive decode step, and explain concretely what state gets
reused between decode steps.

3.2 Why this matters
Every mechanism in the rest of this workbook – attention variants, positional schemes, sparsity, mixtureof-experts routing – is a modification to the same handful of moving parts introduced here: a residual
stream, an attention sublayer, a feed-forward sublayer, and a causal mask. If any of those four feel shaky,
later chapters will read as a list of unrelated tricks rather than variations on one theme. This chapter’s
job is narrow on purpose: get the shared vocabulary and the prefill/decode distinction solid, so that “GQA

land as small, precise edits to a picture you already have, not new pictures from scratch.

3.3 Mental model or analogy
Think of the residual stream as a running total printed on a receipt. Each sublayer in the model doesn’t
erase what’s already printed – it adds one more line of adjustment underneath the current total. By the
time the receipt reaches the bottom of the block, the final total already reflects every earlier adjustment,
because each one was added in, in order.
Analogy
The residual stream is a running total on a receipt. Each sublayer reads the current total, computes an
adjustment, and prints a new line that adds to the total – it never crosses out or overwrites a previous
line. Where this breaks down: a paper receipt keeps each line individually readable, so you can
always look back and see exactly what line 3 contributed. The residual stream cannot do that. Every
adjustment is summed into the same fixed-width vector, so by the end you have one number (well,
one vector) that reflects every contribution – but you cannot read back which sublayer contributed
what just by looking at the final total. That specific limitation is the working assumption behind
why interpretability researchers need extra tools (activation patching, probing) to recover what an
individual sublayer contributed – the sum alone doesn’t show its work.

In words: one more linear projection turns “the model’s final understanding of this position” into one raw

Working approximation
This chapter presents one decoder block’s attention and feed-forward sublayers as the complete
story. A real model stacks 𝐿 of these blocks, each with its own weights, and Chapter 2 will show
that the normalization step glossed over here (Norm in Equation 2) has a specific, consequential form
and placement. Treat everything in this chapter as the shared skeleton that later chapters attach real
components to – it is correct as far as it goes, but it goes only as far as “here is the skeleton every
modern decoder shares.”

Common misconception
“The Transformer generates the entire response in one forward pass.” It does not. One forward
pass produces exactly one next-token distribution, for one additional position. A 50-token response
requires at least 50 forward passes (one prefill pass over the prompt, then one decode step per
generated token) – the model has no mechanism for “seeing ahead” to token 50 while generating
token 2. This misconception is worth taking seriously because it is exactly backward from how latency
actually behaves in production: generation cost scales with response length, almost independently of
how long the prompt was processed in a single prefill pass.

3.10 Interview lens
A common interview framing is: “Walk me through what happens differently between processing a 500token prompt for the first time versus generating the 501st token.” A strong answer distinguishes prefill (all
500 positions processed together, in one pass, building the full KV-cache) from a decode step (one new
position, reusing the 500 cached K/V rows unchanged, only computing K/V for the new token) – and
explicitly rejects “the model re-reads the whole prompt every time,” which is the same misconception as
this chapter’s headline one, just phrased in systems terms. See workbook 02 (ML Interview Practice) for
more on how to structure this kind of answer under time pressure.

3.11 One-page recap
• A decoder-only model conditions strictly on earlier positions; nothing about its objective lets it “see
ahead.”
• The residual stream is additive and constant-width: sublayers add updates to it, they never replace it or
change its dimensionality.
• Attention has three roles (𝑄, 𝐾, 𝑉 ), a causal mask that allows every earlier-or-equal position (not just
the immediately previous one), and a softmax that turns raw scores into a weighted average.
• Prefill processes the whole prompt in one pass and produces cached state; each decode step reuses that
cached state and adds exactly one new position’s worth of new state.
• Generating 𝑁 tokens costs at least 𝑁 forward passes – there is no single-pass shortcut.

4.1 Learning objectives
By the end of this chapter you should be able to:
• Explain RMSNorm’s formula and state precisely what it drops relative to LayerNorm.
• Explain RoPE’s rotation well enough to say why it encodes relative rather than absolute position.
• Explain why a gated feed-forward layer needs three weight matrices instead of two, and what that costs
in parameters.
• For any one of RMSNorm, pre-norm placement, RoPE, SwiGLU, or tied embeddings, say whether it is
an architectural convention (a common but non-forced choice) or something closer to a fundamental
requirement – and justify the answer rather than asserting it.

4.2 Why this matters
Chapter 1 gave you the skeleton every decoder shares: a residual stream, an attention sublayer, a feedforward sublayer, a causal mask. If you opened a 2017 Transformer and a 2025-era open-weight model
side by side, the skeleton would look identical – and almost every detail inside it would be different. This
chapter fills in what actually changed, component by component, and – just as importantly – asks of
each one whether it’s load-bearing or just a common convention. That distinction matters practically:
a convention is something you could swap out without breaking the model’s ability to function; a
requirement is something the architecture depends on. Confusing the two is a fast way to either overexplain a trivial implementation choice or under-explain something that quietly determines a model’s
memory footprint.

Common misconception
“SwiGLU makes the feed-forward block sparse like an MoE layer.” It does not. Every parameter
in a SwiGLU feed-forward layer participates in every token’s forward pass, exactly like the plain
version – the only change is within one dense computation (an extra multiplicative branch), not which
parameters get used. Sparsity, in the sense Chapter 5 will use the word, means a router activates only a
subset of available parameters per token. SwiGLU has no router and activates 100% of its own weights
every time, same as a plain FFN.
Tied vs. untied embeddings. The input embedding table 𝐸 from Equation 1 and the output head 𝑊out
from Equation 6 have transposed shapes (𝑉 × 𝑑model and 𝑑model × 𝑉 ) – some models set 𝑊out = 𝐸 ⊤ (tied
embeddings, one shared table doing both jobs), others learn two separate matrices (untied). Tying halves
that specific parameter cost, at the cost of forcing the same table to serve two different roles (looking a
token up vs. scoring how token-like a hidden vector is).
Implementation-dependent detail
Whether a given model ties its embeddings is a genuine implementation choice that varies across
model families, and this workbook does not have a primary source specifically dedicated to when tying
is preferred (see reports/04_source_audit.md’s tracked gap on this topic). Treat “tied embeddings”
as a real, checkable fact about any specific model’s config – not as a claim this workbook makes a
historical or performance argument about.
Convention vs. requirement, applied. Of everything in this chapter, RoPE-vs-some-other-positionalscheme and tied-vs-untied-embeddings are conventions: a model is a well-defined, functioning decoder
either way, and real, current models make both choices differently. Pre-norm-vs-post-norm is closer to a
requirement-shaped tradeoff at large depth – it is not free to ignore once 𝐿 is large, even though small
models tolerate either. RMSNorm vs. LayerNorm is closest to a pure convention: it changes cost, not what
the model is capable of expressing.
Common misconception
“Every modern decoder uses exactly the same block ordering.” It does not. This chapter’s
components (RMSNorm, pre-norm, RoPE, SwiGLU) describe a common, current combination, not a
single mandatory recipe. Norm placement alone has three real variants in production models (Table 3);
tying is a per-model choice; some models still use post-norm. “Modern decoder” describes a family of
closely related designs, not one design.

4.10 Interview lens
“Why does this model use RoPE instead of learned positional embeddings?” A strong answer states RoPE’s
relative-position property (Equation 8) as the mechanism, names at least one concrete consequence
(no learned positional parameters; different, generally better long-context extrapolation behavior than a
learned table with a fixed max length), and resists the temptation to just say “it’s more modern” without
saying what specifically changed. “Walk me through what SwiGLU changes about the MLP” is answered
well by describing the extra parallel branch and the elementwise multiply (Equation 10), explicitly
including the 1.5x-or-compensated parameter tradeoff from this chapter’s worked example – an answer
that skips the parameter cost entirely is incomplete for a systems-flavored interview.

• Pre-norm, post-norm, and sandwich-norm describe where normalization sits relative to a sublayer and

• RoPE rotates queries and keys by a position-dependent angle inside attention, so their dot product

1. (recall) What does the residual stream carry from one block to the next?
The running sum of the original embedding plus every sublayer update added so far – not just the most
recent sublayer’s raw output. Per the residual update 𝑥(𝑙+1) = 𝑥(𝑙) + 𝑓(Norm (𝑥(𝑙) )), each block adds its
sublayer’s output onto the existing stream value; it never replaces the stream with just the new sublayer’s
output.
Common wrong answer: “the output of the most recent attention or MLP sublayer” – that describes only
the update being added in that step, not the accumulated stream value.
2. (explanation) Why does a decoder-only objective allow one-token- at-a-time generation?
Because the causal mask restricts every position to attending only to itself and earlier positions, the same
forward-pass structure used in training also directly defines how to generate: at each position, produce
a next-token distribution conditioned only on what came before. A bidirectional encoder’s positions can
attend to later positions too, so its representation at a given position already depends on tokens that would
not exist yet during generation – that structure cannot be reused one token at a time the way a causal
one can.
Common wrong answer: “decoder-only models are just smaller/simpler” – size is unrelated; the difference
is specifically about which positions are allowed to attend to which.
3. (calculation) 𝑑model = 8, 𝐻𝑞 = 2, 𝑆 = 5: find 𝑑head and the shape of 𝑄𝐾 ⊤ for one head.
𝑑head = 𝑑model /𝐻𝑞 = 8/2 = 4. The raw attention score matrix for one head is (𝑆, 𝑆) = (5, 5) – one score
per query/key pair, regardless of 𝑑head .

20

Common wrong answer: (𝑆, 𝑑head ) = (5, 4) – this confuses the per-head attention output shape (which is
(𝑆, 𝑑head )) with the score matrix shape (always (𝑆, 𝑆)).
4. (misconception_diagnosis) “Causal masking means each token can only attend to the immediately previous token.”
False. Causal masking allows a position to attend to every position at or before it (𝑗 ≤ 𝑖), not only the
single immediately preceding position. The mask is 0 (allowed) for every 𝑗 ≤ 𝑖 and −∞ (blocked) only
for 𝑗 > 𝑖 – a full lower-triangular region, not a single diagonal band.
Common wrong answer’s origin: conflating causal masking with a fixed local window (sliding-window
attention, a separate mechanism covered in a later chapter). Causal masking alone imposes no limit on
how far back a position can look.
5. (misconception_diagnosis) “The residual stream is a separate memory database the model
writes facts into and reads back later.”
False. The residual stream is a single vector per position, updated additively in place – not a separate,
queryable store. Every sublayer’s output is added directly onto the same fixed-width vector that already
exists at that position; there is no separate storage structure and no mechanism for a later block to
selectively “query” what an earlier block specifically wrote. It can only read the accumulated sum.
Common wrong answer’s origin: taking this chapter’s own receipt analogy too literally – a real receipt’s
lines stay individually readable; the residual stream’s contributions are summed into one vector and
cannot be individually read back out. This is exactly the analogy’s stated breakdown point.

7. (explanation, interview-style) “Walk me through what happens differently between processing a 500-token prompt versus generating the 501st token.”
A strong answer: (1) names prefill – all 500 positions processed together in one pass, building K/V for
every position; (2) names the decode step – exactly one new position processed, computing only its own
new K/V and reusing the 500 cached K/V rows unchanged; (3) explicitly states that generating the 501st
token does not reprocess or re-read the first 500 tokens.
Evaluation criteria: does the answer name both phases correctly, does it state what specifically is reused
(K/V rows) versus recomputed (the one new position’s K/V), and does it avoid the “re-reads the whole
prompt” framing this chapter’s headline misconception warns against?
Weak-answer pattern: “decoding is faster because the prompt is already processed,” without stating what
specifically is reused or why that reuse is what makes it faster.

1. (calculation) RoPE, positions (1,0) and (3,2), both relative offset 1: what’s guaranteed, what
isn’t?
RoPE guarantees the angle between the rotated query and key vectors is identical in both cases, because
each vector is rotated by an amount tied to its own position, so only the relative offset (1, in both cases)
survives. It does not guarantee the dot products are numerically equal in general – the dot product also
depends on the original (pre-rotation) vectors’ magnitudes and directions, which can differ between the
two pairs.
Common overreach: claiming RoPE guarantees identical dot products for any two pairs with the same
relative offset – this conflates the angle (guaranteed to match) with the dot product (which also depends
on vector magnitudes).
2. (compare_and_contrast) Two operations RMSNorm omits relative to LayerNorm.
Mean-centering (subtracting the vector’s mean before rescaling) and the learned bias term (an additive
per-dimension shift after rescaling). LayerNorm computes (𝑥 − mean (𝑥))/ std (𝑥) ⊙ 𝛾 + 𝛽; RMSNorm
computes 𝑥/ RMS (𝑥) ⊙ 𝛾 – no mean subtraction and no 𝛽 term.
Common incomplete answer: naming only the missing bias and missing that mean-centering is also gone
– dropping mean-centering is the more consequential of the two omissions.
3. (explanation) Why three matrices for a gated feed-forward layer?
The third matrix (𝑊3 , the “value” branch) provides a second, purely linear projection of the input that
gets elementwise-multiplied against the first matrix’s activated output (𝑊1 , the “gate” branch) before the
shared output projection (𝑊2 ). 𝑊1 alone only produces one activated signal; 𝑊3 gives something for that
signal to gate.
Common wrong answer: describing the third matrix as making the network “deeper” – it does not; 𝑊1 and
𝑊3 are parallel branches computed from the same input, not a longer sequential chain.

5. (misconception_diagnosis) Pre-norm’s popularity: stability or quality?
Mainly training stability at depth, not final model quality. Pre-norm trades away some representational
capacity for a more stable gradient path through an un-normalized residual stream as 𝐿 grows. Sandwichnorm’s existence (paying for a second normalization step around the same sublayer) is direct evidence
that pre-norm alone is understood to give something up, not that it is a strict improvement.
Common wrong answer: treating pre-norm as simply “the new correct default” with no acknowledged
tradeoff.

6. (misconception_diagnosis) “RoPE adds a learned position vector to every token embedding.”
False. RoPE rotates the query and key vectors inside the attention computation, at every layer that has
attention – it adds nothing to the embedding table, and the rotation angles are fixed by position, not
learned.
Common wrong answer’s origin: conflating RoPE with learned absolute positional embeddings (an older,
different scheme that does add a learned per-position vector directly to the embedding).
7. (misconception_diagnosis) “SwiGLU makes the feed-forward block sparse like an MoE layer.”
False. Every weight in a SwiGLU feed-forward layer is used for every token, exactly like a plain feedforward layer. Sparsity, in the mixture-of-experts sense, means a router activates only a subset of available
parameters per token – SwiGLU has no router and always uses 100% of its own weights.
Common wrong answer’s origin: the word “gated” sounds like it could mean “selecting a subset,” but the
gate here is a continuous, dense elementwise multiply applied to every token, not a discrete routing
decision.
8. (design, applied exercise) Reading a model config excerpt.
Given hidden_size: 2048, num_attention_heads: 16, intermediate_size: 5504, hidden_act: "silu",
rms_norm_eps: 1e-5, tie_word_embeddings: false, rope_theta: 10000.0:
• Normalization: RMSNorm – the rms_norm_eps field name is the giveaway.
• Gated feed-forward: yes – hidden_act: "silu" matches SwiGLU’s Swish/SiLU convention, and
rope_theta separately confirms RoPE is in use.
• Ratio check: 5504/2048 ≈ 2.69, close to 23 × 4 ≈ 2.67 – a gated-compensated ratio, not the plain 4x
ratio.
• Embeddings: untied (tie_word_embeddings: false is explicit).
Common mistake: assuming any roughly-4x intermediate_size implies a plain (ungated) MLP – the
compensated gated ratio is close enough to other plausible ratios that the activation-function field is a
more reliable signal than the ratio alone.
9. (explanation, interview-style) “Why RoPE instead of learned positional embeddings?”
A strong answer states RoPE’s relative-position mechanism as the reason, and names at least one concrete
consequence: no learned positional parameters at all, and generally better behavior beyond the training
length than a learned table with a fixed maximum.
Evaluation criteria: does the answer name the actual mechanism rather than asserting RoPE is “more
modern”; does it name a specific, checkable consequence rather than a vague quality claim?
10. (explanation, interview-style) “Walk me through what SwiGLU changes about the MLP.”
A strong answer describes the extra parallel branch and the elementwise multiply before the shared output
projection, and explicitly states the parameter tradeoff: at matched expansion width, three matrices cost
1.5x what two matrices cost, which is why real models often shrink the expansion width to compensate.
Evaluation criteria: does the answer proactively raise the parameter-cost tradeoff, rather than needing
to be prompted for it – a systems-flavored interviewer will very likely ask a follow-up about cost if the
candidate doesn’t raise it first.
